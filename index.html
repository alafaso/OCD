It was never
about being tidy.

OCD may be the most trivialized diagnosis in everyday language, a word we reach for when a desk is neat or a closet is color-coded. This page follows 100 real patients from a dataset collected at a psychiatrist's office. Their data tells a different story: not a quirk, but a disorder that can shape decades of a life.

02 · what it looks like

It's not cleaning. It's a loop.

Ask someone with OCD what it feels like, and you won't hear “I like a clean kitchen.” You'll hear a loop: an intrusive thought arrives, the anxiety spikes, a ritual fires to shut it down, and the ritual never feels finished, so the loop runs again.

The dataset records each patient's dominant obsession and compulsion. Notice what is actually here: harm. religion. contamination. hoarding. symmetry. There is no “enjoys tidiness” category, symmetry is about dread and incompleteness, not aesthetics.

03 · how long it lasts

04 · comorbidity

It rarely comes alone.

OCD travels with other conditions, and it is treated with real medicine and real therapy, not color-coded binders. Half of these 100 patients have depression on file, and half have a family history of OCD. The dataset was last updated three years ago; for the people in it, the loop was still running.

05 · why I made this

One of these points could be me.

I was diagnosed with OCD pretty late in my life, at the age of 22. For a really long time, I thought I was crazy. I thought I was alone with my thoughts, that I was the only one who thought or functioned this way inside. I floated through life suffering, dealing with my disorder in unhealthy ways that only made my symptoms worse. Until I was diagnosed and started exposure therapy, I was pretty unsure of how anything could possibly get better. I thought life would be that bad forever. That is why I feel the need to highlight other people's cases, not just my own.

07 · the point

Say the whole word: disorder.

OCD is a serious, often lifelong condition, and it is treatable. Exposure and response prevention (ERP) therapy and medication genuinely help. If any of this felt familiar, for you or someone you love, it is worth asking for help. You are not “a little OCD.” And you are not alone.
